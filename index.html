Look closer first

Data analysis

Slow down before deciding what to change. Keep what the data shows apart from what you think it means.

Data Driven Dialogue

A four-phase protocol for looking at results as a group. Predictions, observations and explanations each get their own turn, so the talk stays curious instead of defensive.

1
Predict

Before you look, say what you expect to see. This brings out the assumptions everyone walks in with.

I predict…I assume…I'm wondering if we'll see…
2
Go visual

Put the data where everyone can see it: chart paper, a sublevel bar graph, sticky notes by score.

Where do students cluster?How does the spread differ by mode?
3
Observe

Only what's on the page. No "because" yet. Facts, numbers, patterns.

I observe that…I notice a pattern in…I'm surprised that…
4
Infer

Now explain. Hold more than one explanation and name what else you'd want to know.

I believe the data suggests…Other data that would help…One thing we could try…
You'll leave with: a short list of inferences and one question your team still needs to answer.

Notice / Interpret / Shift

A shorter protocol. Works well for one class or one student.

  1. Notice. Name three things you see in the data. If you catch yourself saying "because," save it for Interpret.
  2. Interpret. What might explain each one? Start with "I wonder if…"
  3. Match. Which of the seven common challenges does this look like?
  4. Shift. Name one change and decide if it's small, medium or large. Then pick an activity from that section of the board to build it out.
You'll leave with: one shift, sized and ready to build out.

Small shift: instruction

Changes you can make in class next week, using the strategies from today.

Tiered Differentiation Plan

Turn one class's score distribution into three groups with different next steps.

  1. Sort students into three groups: below target, at target, above target.
  2. For each group, name what they can do now and the next text type or function to push toward.
  3. Choose one strategy per group: sentence frames, add-another-detail, connecting word cards, because / but / so, 3-sentence story or discourse scramble.

Example

You'll leave with: a plan you can use in class next week.

Tier One Task

Same topic, same context, three versions. Each group is asked for one more step of text type than it produces now.

  1. Choose one speaking or writing task you'll give in the next two weeks.
  2. Write a version for students below, at and above target.
  3. For each version, decide the support and the text type you expect back.
  4. Check that everyone can still share out on the same topic.

Ways to tier

  • Support: frames, word banks, connecting word cards, then none.
  • Text type: sentences, strings, connected sentences, paragraphs.
  • Time frames: present only, then present plus past or future.
  • Function: describe, then compare, then explain or support an opinion.
You'll leave with: one task written three ways.

Medium shift: assessment & curriculum

Change what you ask students to do, how you judge it, and where it shows up in your units.

Level Up a Unit Assessment

Rewrite one speaking or writing task so it gives evidence of your target level, then add a student self-check.

  1. Pick a task from a current unit and name the function it should require.
  2. Rewrite it with the SCRAP framework, then add a level-up option.
  3. Write a 3–4 item student checklist in plain language.

SCRAP framework Catherine Ritz

SSituation. What is happening?A student from another country may study at your school next year.
CChallenge. What problem needs solving?They are choosing between your school and one other.
RRole. Who is the student?A current student at your school.
AAudience. Who is it for?The exchange student.
PProduct / performance. What will students create or do?An email that describes your school, explains why it's a good place to study and asks about their school.

Level-up option: compare your school to the one they attend now. Compare "Describe your school with many details" (slide 198) with this version (slide 199).

Checklist examples

  • I answered the question that was asked.
  • I connected my sentences.
  • I used past tense when telling a story.

Ask yourself

  • Can a student finish it with a list? Then it can only show Novice.
  • Does it ask for more than one time frame? That's the I4+ signal.
You'll leave with: a revised task and a student self-check.

Think-Like-a-Rater Rubric

Raters judge one prompt at one level. Build a rubric for one of your tasks that judges the same way.

  1. Name the task and the one major level it targets.
  2. Use the rater's columns: Meets Fully, Meets Minimally, Some Evidence, No Evidence.
  3. Use what the tests measure as your rows.
  4. Describe each cell in words you could read aloud to students.

Rows to start with

  • Functions and text type
  • Comprehensibility
  • Language control
  • Vocabulary and quality
  • On task
You'll leave with: a rubric for your next speaking or writing task.

Unit Function Inventory

If a function never shows up in a unit, students won't show it on the test.

  1. List the units in one course, in order.
  2. For each unit, name the functions students actually practice and the text type they produce.
  3. Compare to the course target. Circle functions that are missing or appear only once.
  4. Pick one unit to revise first.

Functions by level

  • Novice: name, list, identify, answer simple questions.
  • Intermediate: ask and answer, describe, handle a transaction, begin to narrate.
  • Advanced: narrate in major time frames, explain, compare, handle a complication.
You'll leave with: a course map with gaps marked.

Review Your Rubrics

Bring a rubric you already use and check it against what the proficiency tests look for. Then fix one thing.

  1. Criteria. Does it cover comprehensibility, vocabulary, text type, language control and, for interpersonal tasks, quality of communication? What's missing? What's extra?
  2. One target. Is it written to one target level, or does it try to describe every level at once?
  3. Student language. Could a student read each row and know what to do? Try turning each criterion into a question: "How well am I understood?"
  4. Balance. Does grammar or length outweigh text type? Would a long list score higher than a few connected sentences?
  5. Next steps. Does the rubric tell students how to grow, or only how they scored?

Plan a Department Data Talk

A low-stakes 30–45 minute meeting where your department looks at proficiency results together.

  1. Choose the data and how you'll show it: charts, a gallery walk, one class or the whole program.
  2. Pick a protocol: Data Driven Dialogue, Notice / Interpret / Shift, or a gallery walk (observations, then questions, then takeaways).
  3. Write two or three questions to ask.
  4. Decide on one shared focus to try before the next testing window.

Sample 40-minute agenda

5 minWelcome and norms: curiosity over judgment
5 minPredict
10 minSilent look at the charts, then observations
10 minInferences in small groups
10 minAgree on one shared focus

Show program-level data before any one teacher's class.

You'll leave with: a meeting agenda.

Who else needs to hear it?

Large shifts need people outside your classroom. Plan the conversation, not just the change.

  1. List who needs to know: department, administrators, school committee, families, students.
  2. For each one, pick the single chart or number that tells the story best.
  3. Write the ask in one sentence: time, schedule, testing, or the Seal of Biliteracy at more levels.
  4. Decide who you'll talk to first, and when.

Latin & ALIRA

Classical languages

ALIRA tests interpretive reading only, so these run entirely on reading. Each one matches a breakout from today.

Level It, Then Ask It

Sort real texts onto the ALIRA scale, then write the kind of question ALIRA asks.

Part 1: Level the texts

  1. Read each text in the set below (A–G) for gist. Don't translate.
  2. Place each at Novice, Intermediate or Advanced.
  3. Say what makes it that level: length, sentence structure, familiar topic, support from pictures or context.

The text set

AFour picture cards, each with a one-line Latin caption ("Puella in horto ambulat.")Teacher-made; images from Wikimedia Commons
BA list of six classroom or household rules in LatinTeacher-made
CA short dialogue from Plautus, MenaechmiPerseus Catalog
DA Pompeii wall inscription, CIL 4.5296 (a few lines)Wikipedia
EOne Martial epigram (4–6 lines)The Latin Library
FPliny the Younger, Letters 6.16 (Vesuvius), one paragraphThe Latin Library
GA modern Latin news storyEphemeris

Part 2: Ask it like ALIRA

  1. Choose one text, or a passage you already teach.
  2. Write two four-option questions in English: one main idea, one supporting detail.
  3. For each wrong answer, note why someone would pick it.

Part 3: Swap and test

  • Can it be answered without translating the whole passage?
  • Can it be answered by spotting one or two words? Then it tests vocabulary.
  • Would each wrong answer tempt someone who half understood?
  • Does the question match the text's level?
You'll leave with: one leveled text and two ALIRA-style questions.

Translation or Comprehension?

ALIRA asks students to get at meaning without translating, so a program built only on translation can produce strong students with low scores.

  1. Rater lens. I-2, I-3 and I-4 are all Intermediate Mid. What would Sally need to read consistently to reach I-4? I-5?
  2. Family lens. Rewrite the I-2 descriptor in two plain sentences a parent would understand. Add one thing to do at home.
  3. Student lens. Use the coaching stem "You showed really strong evidence of ___" with the report's strategies: cognates, titles and pictures, prior knowledge, rereading.

Latin common challenges

  • Strong grammar, low score: knowing forms isn't reading on sight. See Challenge #2.
  • Stalled at I-1/I-2: mostly textbook stories, rarely authentic or modern texts. See #3.
  • "Ran out of time": translated in their heads instead of reading for gist. See #6.

Latin shifts to start from

SmallSwap one translation assignment for comprehension questions. Open class with a short sight passage read for gist. Use embedded (tiered) readings of the same story.
MediumAdd one ALIRA-style interpretive task to every unit assessment. Bring modern Latin texts and dialogues into each level. Use some spoken Latin input to build reading.
LargeSet reading targets by level with the NYSED classical indicators. Give ALIRA as a pre/post test and define what growth means locally. Bring Latin into the Seal of Biliteracy conversation.
